Open the manuscript and it divides into six visual worlds.

The largest section resembles an herbal. Each page presents a plant, but many roots, leaves, and flowers resist reliable identification. Next come astronomical and astrological pages: suns, stars, zodiac-like rings, and circular diagrams that almost resemble calendars.

A third section shows small human figures linked by pools, pipes, and flowing shapes. Curators call it biological or balneological—connected with bathing—but that label describes the imagery, not its meaning. Cosmological foldouts expand into elaborate medallions. Pharmaceutical pages pair plant parts with vessel-like objects. The final pages look like short recipes, each entry marked by a star-shaped flower.

Every image feels one step away from recognition. That is the trap. A plant can look medicinal without matching a species. A circle can look astronomical without mapping a known sky. Because the labels cannot be read, pictures cannot securely explain the words—and the words cannot securely explain the pictures.
