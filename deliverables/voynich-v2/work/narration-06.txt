Four broad explanations remain in contention.

Theory one: an ordinary language hidden by substitution, abbreviation, or a complex cipher. The regularity fits—but no proposed key produces a coherent reading across the whole manuscript.

Theory two: a lost shorthand or invented alphabet. This could explain why the text looks linguistic while resisting familiar scripts. Without a parallel text, it is extremely difficult to test.

Theory three: a constructed notation meaningful only to a small community—perhaps medical, religious, or scholarly. Five scribal hands make a shared convention plausible, but they do not reveal its purpose.

Theory four: an elaborate hoax. Experiments show that rule-based nonsense can imitate some Voynich statistics. But “meaningless” does not explain the costly parchment, sustained artwork, multiple writers, or the intended buyer. A hoax would still be a remarkable organized production.

Each theory explains one clue and collides with another. Cipher explains secrecy, but not the missing key. Language explains structure, but not identity. Hoax explains failure—but not effort.
