The ownership trail begins in imperial Prague. Yale identifies Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf the Second as the manuscript's first confirmed owner. A surviving account says he paid six hundred gold ducats and believed the work might be by the English thinker Roger Bacon. Modern scholarship treats that attribution as speculation.

The book later passed through the library of pharmacist Jacobus de Tepenec and then to Prague alchemist Georgius Baresch. Baresch called it a riddle and asked the celebrated Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher for help. In 1666, Johannes Marcus Marci sent the manuscript to Kircher in Rome with a letter still preserved inside it.

Then, silence. For more than two centuries, the trail becomes uncertain.

In 1912, Polish-American rare-book dealer Wilfrid Voynich purchased it from a Jesuit college near Rome. The mystery took his name. After passing through later owners, bookseller H. P. Kraus donated it to Yale in 1969.

Today every page is online. Access is no longer the problem. Interpretation is.
